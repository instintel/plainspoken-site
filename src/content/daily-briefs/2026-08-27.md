Somewhere in a finance team's inbox this morning sits a live decision: lock in today's ringgit rate, or wait out the next 48 hours. That's the actual choice a fair number of us are weighing right now, because the ringgit just touched its strongest level in about two months — for reasons that have almost nothing to do with anything Malaysia did this week.

Start with what actually happened across the window. Bursa reopened on Wednesday after Tuesday's Prophet Muhammad's Birthday holiday, and the FBM KLCI climbed 12.21 points to 1,748.54, up 0.7%, snapping a two-day slide on the back of banking counters and healthy market breadth (777 gainers against 466 decliners). The ringgit strengthened alongside it, closing at 4.0240 against the dollar — its best print in roughly two months — up from Monday's 4.0405. On Wall Street, the session that closed just as our window ran out looked quiet on the surface: the Dow off 113 points, the S&P 500 down a bare 0.1%, the Nasdaq essentially flat. Underneath, two forces were pulling in different directions — a headline inflation reading that came in a touch hotter than expected (the Fed's preferred gauge rose 3.7% annually against a forecast of 3.6%, even as the "core" version, which strips out food and energy, landed exactly on forecast at 3.3%), and Nvidia's earnings, released just after the closing bell, which most of the trading day had spent quietly waiting for.

The number that matters isn't the earnings print itself, tempting as that is. Nvidia's revenue came to $96.2 billion for the quarter, more than double what it was a year ago, and its data-centre business alone grew 117% to $89 billion — both ahead of what analysts had pencilled in. What matters more is what the company itself projected for next quarter: $108 billion. Divide one figure into the other and you get the size of the bar Nvidia has now set for itself — roughly 12% growth quarter-on-quarter, stacked on top of a base that's already the largest of any semiconductor company in history. Picture a runner who keeps smashing their own personal best, only to find the finish line has quietly moved another hundred metres back each time — a very simplistic illustration, obviously, but it's roughly the position Nvidia's shareholders are in. Every quarter this cycle, the size of "good enough" gets larger. That's worth sitting with, because a version of the same dynamic, at a different scale, is playing out in the ringgit.

Here's the confrontation worth having with ourselves: the instinct, seeing a two-month-high ringgit and a calmer Bursa, is to read it as Malaysia doing something right. Fair enough where it's true — the banking sector's gains and the healthy market breadth are genuinely domestic. But the currency move is a different animal entirely. It's being driven by traders betting that the Fed, under its new chair Kevin Warsh, sounds dovish at the Jackson Hole symposium that opens today and runs through Saturday. The reporting is explicit that the odds of a September rate rise are being priced as low — not that anyone in Kuala Lumpur has changed the underlying calculus.

If you run a business with dollar receivables or payables, here's something worth ten minutes today rather than a vague resolution to "watch the ringgit." Pull your net unhedged US-dollar exposure — the actual figure, not the one your gut tells you it roughly is. Multiply it by 4.0240, then multiply that again by the roughly 0.4% swing the ringgit has already shown between Monday and Wednesday this week alone. That gives you the ringgit value currently at stake from perfectly ordinary week-to-week movement, before Jackson Hole has even opened its mouth. If that number would keep you up at night, that's information about your hedging position, not about the market.

The second-order point is the one worth carrying past today. Two separate clocks are ticking over Malaysia at once this week, and they don't run on the same mechanism. One is the rate-cycle clock — ringgit strength, bond inflows, the calculus around Jackson Hole — which is almost entirely a function of what happens in Washington, not Kuala Lumpur. The other is the capex-cycle clock: the same AI infrastructure spending behind Nvidia's number is the thing filling data-centre land in Kulim and Johor, and that cycle isn't obviously listening to what the Fed does at all — it kept accelerating through a stretch of high rates already. A business owner glancing at a strong ringgit and concluding "good time to import capital equipment" may be reading the first clock correctly while missing that the second one — the one shaping land prices, power tariffs and skilled-labour costs regionally — is running on an entirely separate schedule.

What would change this view: if the new Fed chair's remarks on Friday lean hawkish — pointing to that hotter 3.7% headline print as reason for caution rather than dismissing it — the ringgit's two-month high could unwind within days, because it was priced on an expectation, not a commitment. On the other side, a visible deceleration in data-centre revenue growth next quarter, meaningfully below that implied 12%, would be the clearest signal that the capex clock is finally slowing down to meet the rate clock, rather than running ahead of it.

What to watch, with dates: the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium itself, running 27–29 August, with the Fed chair's remarks expected on Friday, 28 August; and Nvidia's following quarterly report later this year, which will be the cleanest available read on whether data-centre growth is still accelerating or has begun to plateau.

None of this calls for anything dramatic before Friday. It's closer to the older discipline of knowing our own numbers well enough that someone else's headline can't rattle a decision we've already made calmly. So the real question isn't what the Fed chair says on Friday — it's whether you already know your own exposure well enough for it not to matter either way?
