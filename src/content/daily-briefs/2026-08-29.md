By Friday teatime, our ringgit had already made up its mind about Kevin Warsh. It closed at 4.0230 to the US dollar, a touch firmer than Thursday's 4.0305, on the assumption — reasonable enough at the time — that the Fed's newest chairman would use his first Jackson Hole keynote to keep policy vague rather than pick a fight with inflation. Bursa Malaysia had its own, unrelated worry: traders sold into the long Merdeka weekend regardless, and the FBM KLCI slipped 0.91% to 1,725.88, losers outnumbering gainers nearly two to one on heavy volume. Both markets shut for the day. Warsh hadn't opened his mouth yet.

!(/images/daily-briefs/2026-08-29/chart-03-klci-breadth.png)

He did a few hours later, in Jackson Hole's thin mountain air, once our shops had already put up the bunting. And he was not vague. "We have work to do," he said of inflation still running above the Fed's 2% target, with July's core reading at 3.3%. The market did the only thing a market can do with a sentence like that: it repriced, fast. The odds of a rate rise at the Fed's 15–16 September meeting, tracked through CME's futures market, jumped from roughly a third to a coin flip — 59%, up from 35% the day before, inside a single speech. Wall Street, still trading when the words landed, felt it immediately: the Nasdaq gave back 0.5%, the S&P 0.3%, the Dow was essentially flat. All three still closed out the week higher, courtesy of Thursday's Nvidia-led surge, but Friday read as a correction to that surge, not a continuation of it. PayPal did the rest of the day's damage on its own, down 11.9% after Stripe and Advent walked away from a roughly $50 billion take-private deal — unrelated to Warsh, worth knowing about on its own terms.

!(/images/daily-briefs/2026-08-29/chart-02-fed-hike-odds.png)

The number that matters is that jump: 35% to 59%. It's a measure of how quickly a market's working assumption can flip. A week ago, the base case among traders was that the Fed's next move, if any, was down. One hawkish half-hour put "up" back on the table as the coin-flip favourite. That is not a forecast we'd make with any confidence, and Warsh didn't make one publicly either. It is simply the honest state of uncertainty the market is now pricing, and that uncertainty is the useful fact here, not a prediction of where rates land.

If you run a business here, the ten-minute version of this is a debt-servicing check, not a currency call. Pull whatever US-dollar facility your company carries — trade financing, a revolving credit line, anything that reprices off SOFR — and find its next reset date. Compare the forward curve your bank is quoting today against whatever assumption you built into this year's budget. If that budget assumed the Fed kept cutting, the gap between that assumption and today's curve is wider than it was a week ago. Worth knowing by how much before the reset lands, not after.

The second order is a sequencing problem specific to us. Bank Negara's own rate-setting committee meets this coming Thursday, 3 September, nearly two weeks before the Fed does. Our central bank has to decide on the ringgit's home turf without knowing which way Washington will finally go. It's a little like a jury being asked for its verdict during the lunch recess, before the star witness has finished testifying — a very simplistic illustration, obviously, since Bank Negara isn't adjudicating the Fed and answers to its own domestic mandate first. But the sequencing is real this time: a hawkish surprise landing five days before our own decision, rather than five weeks.

The obvious read of Friday's ringgit strength is that markets here are calm about all this. What it actually shows is that our market closed five hours before the information that mattered arrived. That isn't calm. It's just early.

What would change this view: the US jobs report for August lands Friday, 4 September, the day after Bank Negara's meeting and a week and a half before the Fed's. If it repeats July's soft surprise — the one that had pushed hike odds down in the first place — Friday's hawkish repricing likely unwinds part of the way, and the coin flip drifts back toward "no change." That is the single number most likely to move this story again before the Fed itself does.

What to watch, with dates:

Monday, 31 August — Bursa and Malaysian banks closed for Merdeka Day, so Friday's repricing doesn't show up here until Tuesday.
Thursday, 3 September — Bank Negara's Monetary Policy Committee meets, deciding without knowing the Fed's next move.
Friday, 4 September — the US August jobs report, the next data point that could swing the coin flip either way.
Tuesday–Wednesday, 15–16 September — the FOMC meeting itself, with a fresh set of economic projections attached.

None of it rewards moving fast on a guess so much as knowing exactly when your own information has gone stale.

The honest — and interesting — question, perhaps, isn't whether the ringgit was right on Friday, but whether we'll notice, ourselves, the next time our market prices something before the person who actually decides it has finished speaking. Hold that thought until Bursa reopens on Tuesday.
