The story doing the rounds this week is that a fresh round of US-Iran strikes has rattled an otherwise calm oil market. That's not quite what's happening. The Strait of Hormuz has been mostly closed to commercial shipping since the last week of February — today is roughly its 186th day shut — and what's kept it shut for most of that stretch isn't really the fighting. It's insurance. Quotes for war-risk cover on a passage through the strait vary a fair bit by source and by vessel, but they cluster in a wide, ugly range: somewhere between fifteen and forty times pre-crisis levels, commonly US$5 million to US$10 million or more to underwrite a single large crude tanker for the transit. It's a bit like a landlord who won't relist a flood-damaged unit even months after the water's gone, because no insurer will write a policy until they've seen a dry season first — a very simplistic parallel, obviously, but the mechanism is the same. A ship owner doesn't need to see a warship to stay away. A number on a quote does the job just as well.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-03/chart-02-eia-inventory-draw.png)

That distinction matters for how we read this week's numbers. On Wednesday, Brent traded in the mid-US$90s, still roughly 30% above where it sat before February, while the US government's own weekly inventory count, released the same day, showed something sturdier than a risk premium: US crude stockpiles fell 4.5 million barrels, about four times the size of the draw analysts had expected, and refineries ran at 98% of capacity. Wall Street still closed higher on the day, all three major indexes up, snapping two losing sessions, and Bursa Malaysia added 0.48% to close at 1,708.74. We'd concede the obvious point: none of that looks like a market in crisis. But a market can price real, ongoing tightness calmly for a long stretch before it doesn't, and a physical draw four times consensus, sitting on top of a strait that's been shut for six months, is not nothing.

The second, quieter story this week sits nine time zones away from Hormuz, in Tokyo. Japan's 10-year government bond yield touched 3% on Tuesday for the first time since 1996 - thirty years - and it's stayed roughly there since. Japanese investors have responded the way most of us would to a suddenly more attractive yield at home: they've sold about ¥3 trillion of foreign bonds so far this year, net, the largest such pullback since 2022. For three decades, Japan's own savings pool has been one of the world's biggest buyers of foreign debt, US Treasuries very much included — a buyer who kept turning up regardless of the price on offer. A buyer that size going quiet doesn't announce itself with a headline. It just shows up, later, as a slightly higher yield on everything else.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-03/chart-01-hormuz-insurance-multiplier.png)

The number that matters is the insurance multiplier on a Hormuz transit - reported anywhere from 15 to 40 times pre-crisis levels depending on the source and the day, commonly several million dollars and sometimes into eight figures per large tanker. It functions as a tax that no government collects and that won't expire on a ceasefire announcement, because underwriters reprice risk on evidence of sustained safe passage, not on a single quiet week.

If you run a business here, the ten-minute version: if any part of your supply chain moves through a chartered vessel via the Gulf, ask your freight forwarder for today's war-risk insurance quote on that route as a percentage of cargo value, and compare it against the quote from three months back. That gap is now doing to your landed cost roughly what a tariff would, without ever showing up as one on an invoice.

The second order worth sitting with: global bond yields can keep climbing even if the US Federal Reserve does precisely nothing this month. Japan's own domestic yields are pulling its own capital home, and a buyer of that scale disappearing from the market for foreign bonds pushes the going rate up for every other borrower competing for the capital that's left — governments, companies, and anyone in Malaysia pricing a facility off a global benchmark, whether or not Bank Negara moves an inch.

What would change this view: a real, sustained fall in Hormuz war-risk insurance premiums - evidence that underwriters are pricing in safer passage, not just a quieter week of headlines - would be the clearest sign the physical closure is genuinely easing. The falsifier runs the other way too: if Japanese institutions resume net-buying of foreign bonds even with JGB yields still near 3%, the capital-repatriation story here would need rethinking.

What to watch, with dates:

- **Thursday 3 September (today), 3:00pm** - Bank Negara Malaysia's Monetary Policy Statement; consensus expects the Overnight Policy Rate, which anchors borrowing costs across the country, held at 2.75%.
- **Monday 7 September** - US markets closed for Labor Day.
- **Tuesday 15–Wednesday 16 September** - the US Federal Reserve's next rate decision.
- **Ongoing** - Hormuz war-risk insurance quotes and Bursa Malaysia's own market breadth, both of which said more about this week than the headline index numbers did.

Cheap capital has been the default assumption for most of our working lives; the discipline this particular week asks for is pricing as if that's no longer a safe thing to assume, for reasons that have nothing to do with our own creditworthiness.

Regardless of whether the Fed moves this month - would the world still have a buyer left who doesn't care about the price, to keep a lid on yields when the Fed doesn't? Watch Tokyo before Washington.
