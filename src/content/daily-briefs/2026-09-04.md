This week, if you're the one deciding whether to lock in a business loan's floating rate before Monday's treasury call, here's where things actually stand: one central bank has already made up its mind, and the other is still working it out in public.

Bank Negara held its overnight policy rate at 2.75% on Thursday, its seventh straight meeting without a change, exactly as 20 of 22 economists surveyed by Bloomberg expected. The central bank pointed to growth of 5.7% in the first half of the year, a full-year forecast near 5%, and inflation still contained — headline at 1.8%, core at 2.0% through July. Hours later, on the other side of the Pacific, Fed Governor Christopher Waller broke from Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole message a week earlier, saying he'd support holding US rates steady at the 15-16 September meeting if the inflation data due before then keeps cooling — "give disinflation a chance," as he put it, "we can wait one meeting." Traders who'd pushed the odds of a September hike into the low-to-mid 60% range earlier in the week brought them back down toward roughly a coin flip. Wall Street liked the news: the Dow added around 635 points, roughly 1.2%, with the S&P and Nasdaq both up over a full percent. Oil stayed a touch firmer than usual — Brent near $95-97, WTI near $91-93 — with US-Iran tensions back in the headlines after a quieter stretch. Bursa Malaysia closed Thursday up 0.37% at 1,715.13, gainers outnumbering losers 625 to 497.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-04/chart-01-us-indices.png)

Warsh's warning a week ago had a fair point underneath it — headline US inflation is still running near 3.7%, core near 3.3%, comfortably above target, and a Fed chair whose job is price stability doesn't get to look past that. What Thursday showed is that his own board doesn't share his urgency, at least not yet, and the market moved on that almost as soon as he said it. It's a bit like one department head, in a meeting the chief executive didn't attend, telling the room the year-end numbers might land fine after all — and watching the mood shift before the CEO has said a word. A very simple way of putting it, obviously; Waller's comments move markets, not one budget line, but the mechanism runs the same way.

The number worth sitting with today isn't the Fed's odds swing, though — it's a quieter one tucked inside Bank Negara's own statement. First-half growth came in at 5.7%. The full-year forecast is "around 5%." Do the simple maths and that points to growth closer to 4.3% in the second half — a full percentage point slower than what we've just delivered. That's our own reading of two numbers Bank Negara published side by side, not a forecast the central bank spelled out itself. It's not a crisis number. It's a business-planning one, worth checking against what's actually sitting in the budget.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-04/chart-02-growth-h1-h2.png)

Here's the ten-minute version: pull your own firm's revenue or order-book growth for the first half of 2026 against the same period last year, then look at what growth rate your current budget assumes for the second half. If the wider economy is expected to slow by close to a point and your plan still assumes acceleration, that's worth a conversation with finance this week, rather than a surprise at year-end.

There's a nice bit of continuity here, too. We asked on 29 August whether Bank Negara would be forced to decide blind, nearly two weeks ahead of the Fed's own meeting. It has now answered that — it held, on domestic growth and inflation numbers that stand on their own, rather than as a hedge against whatever Washington does next. Thursday's KLCI gain still moved with the broader mood the Fed and oil set for markets everywhere this week, so it's not complete independence, but Bank Negara's own calendar clearly isn't waiting on the Fed's.

What would change this view: a hot US inflation report on 11 September, followed by a Fed hike regardless of Thursday's rally, would widen the rate gap between Washington and Kuala Lumpur right when Bank Negara has signalled it's comfortable standing still. The other thing worth watching is whether calm around the Strait of Hormuz actually holds, or whether this week turns out to be more than a passing flare-up.

What to watch, with dates:

- **11 September** - the US August inflation report, the data point Waller himself named as decisive for his vote
- **15-16 September** - the Fed's next rate-setting meeting and decision
- **Ongoing** - whether the Gulf stays calm, or this week's flare-up turns into something more sustained

The discipline worth keeping through a week like this is separating what moved money today from what's actually going to matter in a month. Have we run that H1-versus-H2 comparison against our own numbers yet, or is that still on the to-do list?
