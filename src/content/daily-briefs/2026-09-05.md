Yesterday's edition described Wall Street's sharpest rally in weeks, built on Fed Governor Christopher Waller's suggestion that the US central bank could hold rates steady this month. That rally lasted about a day. Friday's August jobs report landed at 162,000 new positions, against a consensus clustered around 55,000 to 56,000 — nearly three times what economists had pencilled in — and the reaction was immediate: yields rose, and the three major US indices gave back a slice of Thursday's gains. The Dow closed down 271.86 points, or 0.5%, at 53,414.25; the S&P 500 fell 0.4% to 7,718.60; the Nasdaq eased 0.3% to 26,506.99. Unemployment held steady at 4.1%.

None of this is a crisis. It's a reminder that one governor's comment and one data release can point in opposite directions within twenty-four hours, and markets will follow whichever one they're looking at that day.

Closer to home, the FBM KLCI closed Friday at 1,708.10, down 0.41%, having climbed from 1,700.54 on Wednesday to 1,715.13 on Thursday before Friday's report ended that short run. Breadth was thin either way — 523 gainers against 568 losers, 596 unchanged. Dealers pointed to profit-taking in plantation counters after a strong run in palm oil prices, alongside continued foreign selling and softer crude. The ringgit slipped a touch too, closing at 4.0425/0465 against the dollar versus Thursday's 4.0405/0445 — a small move, but one clearly tracking the same US rate debate that moved everything else this week.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-05/chart-02-klci-weekly-trend.png)

The number that matters today isn't Friday's rate-hike odds, which have swung around too much this week to trust any single reading. It's that this is the fourth month this year payrolls have missed consensus by more than 100,000 in either direction — a 150,000 shortfall in February, a 113,000 beat in March, a 108,000 shortfall in July, and now a 107,000 beat in August. A model that misses by that much, that often, isn't having a bad month. It's reading something wrong about how this labour market actually moves, and that's worth remembering the next time one release feels like the final word.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-05/chart-01-nfp-surprise-history.png)

If you run a business here, the ten-minute version of this is a hiring and financing stress-test, not a Fed prediction. Pull up whatever hiring or capital-spending plan assumes a particular rate path over the next quarter, and run it twice: once assuming the Fed holds at the 15-16 September meeting, once assuming it moves. If the difference between those two scenarios would change whether you proceed, the plan was built on more certainty than the data currently supports — worth knowing before the decision, not after.

The second-order point here is about waiting. It's tempting to treat weeks like this one as a reason to delay a currency-hedging or borrowing decision until "the picture is clearer." But the picture went from a coin-flip to a hawkish lean and back within about two trading sessions, and there's no reason to expect it to sit still between now and the FOMC meeting. A very simplistic illustration, obviously, but it's a bit like waiting for a calm moment to cross a busy street — the traffic doesn't pause because we'd prefer it to. The more useful habit is planning for a range of outcomes rather than the single one that feels most likely this week.

What would change this view: a soft August CPI print would probably undo Friday's hawkish lean the way Thursday's rally got undone — inflation, not jobs, is what the Fed has said repeatedly it's watching most closely into September. If CPI surprises to the downside, expect this week's rate-hike narrative to fade again just as quickly as it appeared.

What to watch, with dates:

- **Friday, 11 September** - US August CPI report, five days ahead of the Fed decision
- **Tuesday–Wednesday, 15–16 September** - FOMC meeting and rate decision
- **Monday, 7 September** - next Bursa Malaysia session, first read on whether Friday's profit-taking continues

We've been here before, in smaller versions, most weeks this year: a data point moves the Fed narrative, and the narrative moves everything downstream of it, ringgit included. The Fed's decision on 16 September is out of our hands. Whether the plans we've built can bear either outcome isn't. Hold that thought until the CPI print on 11 September.
