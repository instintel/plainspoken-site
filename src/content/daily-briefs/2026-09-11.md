The working rule most trading desks have gone by this week: when the odds of a US rate hike rise, the ringgit gives up some ground, because higher US yields pull money back toward the dollar. That's usually a fair read - just not quite what happened this time.

Here's what went down. Thursday's wholesale inflation numbers came in hot, with the headline print at 5.4% year-on-year against a forecast around 5.3%, and core prices at their highest since June. Traders pushed the odds of a hike at next week's Fed meeting to about 70%, roughly eight points higher than before the report. Oil didn't help either. Brent climbed toward US$108 a barrel, its highest since July, after Iranian strikes hit US military aircraft at a base in Jordan overnight, and tensions in the region are running higher again. Wall Street had a rough day too: the Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq all closed lower for a fourth straight session, and the ten-year US government bond yield hit its highest point in a year. Our own market felt the same nerves. Bursa snapped three flat sessions to close down 0.44% at 1,706.82, with losers well ahead of gainers.

And yet our ringgit firmed. It closed at 4.0630/0670 against the US dollar, a touch stronger than Wednesday's 4.0670/0720, and stronger against the Singapore dollar too. Picture a treasurer with two screens open this week, one tracking the Fed odds and one tracking the ringgit, watching them just refuse to agree. A pretty simple picture, obviously, since half the desks in the region are staring at the same two screens right now, not just ours.

The number that matters isn't the 70% on its own. It's the eight-point jump behind it, since that's the part that's supposed to move a currency and didn't. If we're carrying any US dollar exposure, say a loan, an import contract, or equipment financing, it's worth doing the ten-minute version today: take the outstanding balance, add a further quarter-point to whatever portion floats, and see what that does to next year's financing cost. Small number for most of us on our own. Not so small if nobody's actually run it.

The more interesting question is why our currency didn't move the way the textbook says it should. One candidate answer: we pulled in a record US$3.9 billion in foreign bond inflows in August, largely on optimism about our role in the AI and data-centre cycle. If a good chunk of that money hasn't been reallocated on every shift in Fed odds, the calm probably isn't the market disbelieving Washington. It's likely just capital that arrived for a different reason and hasn't had one to leave yet.

What would change this view: if the ringgit weakens meaningfully after Friday's US inflation print, or in the run-up to the Fed's decision, that would say this week's calm was a lull, not real resilience. It would also mean our bond-inflow cushion is thinner than it looks.

What to watch, with dates:

- **Friday, 11 September (evening MYT)**: the US August inflation report, the last big number before the Fed decision.
- **Tuesday-Wednesday, 15-16 September**: the Fed meets, decision expected on the 16th.

The habit worth keeping here is simple: checking, every time the odds move this sharply, whether our own numbers were quietly assuming they wouldn't. The odds moved eight points in one session this week. If Friday's number moves them again, would ours still hold?
