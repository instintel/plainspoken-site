While the local bourse was shut for Malaysia Day, the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate for the first time since July 2023, by 25 basis points, to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%, on a unanimous 12-0 vote. Chair Kevin Warsh was blunt about why: "The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long." US consumer prices rose 3.4% in the year to August, the same pace as July. The committee's own projections now carry 1 more hike before the year is out, then a hold through 2027.

Wall Street naturally reacted. The Dow fell roughly 631 points, about 1.2%, and the S&P 500 lost around 0.45%, while the Nasdaq Composite finished close to flat. That split is worth noticing, because the index most exposed to long-duration growth stories was the one that did not flinch. The dollar index pushed above 100 to a 7-week high, and oil went the other way, with Brent easing about 3% toward $105.50 as supply worries around the Gulf cooled. Our own market reopens this morning from Tuesday's close of 1,679.21, down 18.80 points that day, with 2 sessions of news to price in one.

Here is the number that matters, and it appears on nobody's press release. Take Malaysia's Overnight Policy Rate, held at 2.75% since July last year, and subtract headline inflation of 1.8% in the year to July. That leaves a real policy rate of about 0.95 percentage points. Now do the same for the United States after last night: the midpoint of the new target range is 3.875%, inflation is 3.4%, and the real policy rate is about 0.48 percentage points. Malaysia, which nobody would describe as fighting inflation and whose central bank has not moved in 14 months, is running money roughly twice as restrictive in real terms as the country that just tightened. The two inflation readings sit a month apart, Malaysia's July against America's August, because our statistics office publishes later; August's Malaysian number lands tomorrow morning and will move this gap one way or the other.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-17/chart-01-inflation-divergence.png)

That is not a criticism of Bank Negara, and the case for holding is a reasonable one. Growth ran at 5.7% in the first half, headline and core inflation averaged 1.8% and 2% over the first 7 months, and a central bank with those numbers has no obvious reason to do anything. But holding a rate is a decision about the rate, not about how much restraint the economy is actually carrying. Restraint is what is left after prices are taken out, and that residue has been drifting without anyone touching the lever.

If you run a business here, this is a 10-minute exercise with an actual number at the end of it. Open your single largest US dollar obligation falling due in the next 12 months: a supplier contract, a lease, a tranche of borrowing, a licence fee. Price it twice, once at Tuesday's ringgit close of 4.0835 and once at 4.20. The second figure is about 2.8% higher. Then look at the margin on whatever that obligation supports and work out whether 2.8% lives inside the line or eats it. Most currency commentary will not give you a figure you can put in a cell.

The second order is where the reflex misleads. Everyone's instinct on a Fed hike is that the ringgit weakens, because capital moves toward the higher rate. The nominal gap between the 2 policy rates is now 1.125 percentage points, widened from 0.875 overnight, and on that reading the instinct holds. In real terms, though, the ringgit pays more rather than less: money held here loses roughly half a percentage point less ground to prices over a year than money held there loses to American prices. A very simplistic illustration, obviously, since almost nobody's exposure is a deposit left alone for a year. It still points at something. If capital leaves anyway, it is chasing the nominal carry rather than the real return, and capital that chases nominal carry is the sort that leaves quickly when the carry moves again. Hold that against August's record bond inflows. The question was never whether the money arrived. It was what the money was buying.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-17/chart-02-real-policy-rates.png)

What would change this view is published tomorrow. If August inflation comes in much above 2.25%, Malaysia's real policy rate drops below America's and this entire reading inverts without anybody making a decision. That would be a larger 1-month jump than 2026 has produced so far, since the range has run between 1.4% and 2.0% all year, which is what makes it a test rather than a hedge.

What to watch, with dates:

- **Friday, 18 September**: the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) publishes August CPI.
- **Wednesday, 30 September**: US August PCE, the Fed's own preferred gauge, last read at 3.7% for July.
- **Early October**: Budget 2027 is tabled, which is where the fiscal side of the same cost picture becomes visible.
- **27–28 October**: the next FOMC meeting, which futures pricing currently splits close to evenly.
- **Thursday, 5 November**: Bank Negara's final MPC of 2026, and the only scheduled date on which the Malaysian side of this gap can move.

A policy rate is a decision. The cost of money is an outcome, and the two only occasionally agree. The OPR has not moved in 14 months, and the cost of money here has changed anyway. If it changes again tomorrow morning without anybody deciding anything, would our own 2027 numbers still hold?
