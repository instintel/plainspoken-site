4.6% is how much of the money foreign investors pulled out of Malaysian equities over the past 6 weeks came back last week. The weekly fund-flow tally published on Monday showed foreign funds as net buyers of RM127.0m in the week to 18 September, the first net inflow since the end of July, after 6 straight weeks of net selling that added up to about RM2.78b between 3 August and 11 September.

Set that beside what the country has been reporting over the same stretch. Exports rose 45.5% year on year in August to RM191.0b, the monthly trade surplus widened to RM28.1b, and the surplus for January to August, at RM198.7b, was more than double the same period a year earlier. Foreign investors also put US$3.9b into Malaysian government and corporate bonds in August, the largest monthly inflow in the central bank's data going back to 2016. In that same month they sold about RM2b of local equities.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-22/chart-03-august-exports-year-on-year.png)

Both of those things are true, and on the face of it they shouldn't be. If the export numbers are believed well enough to set a bond-buying record, we would expect the listed market to be sharing in it.

Monday itself was quiet by comparison. The FBM KLCI rose 1.38 points, or 0.08%, to 1,666.94, carried by the banks, while the mid-cap FBM70 fell 0.87%. The ringgit firmed to 4.0755 against the US dollar from 4.0795 as oil eased on renewed talk of US-Iran contact at the United Nations. Wall Street then had its best day since early August: the Nasdaq Composite rose 2.26% to 27,122.09 and the S&P 500 1.49% to 7,764.70, led by chipmakers, with Intel up about 12% and AMD about 10%. Brent settled a little above $100 a barrel, down roughly 3.4%, and the US 10-year yield slipped about 4bp to around 4.95%. Local markets see all of that for the first time this morning.

**The number in highlight**

The 4.6% is RM127.0m divided by the roughly RM2,776.6m that left in the 6 weeks before it. It measures listed equities only and says nothing about bonds, which is exactly where the gap sits, and a single week is a thin sample, so it reads as a direction rather than a turn. What it does is set the scale: foreign money has come back to local shares, and it has come back with loose change.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-22/chart-01-weekly-foreign-equity-flows.png)

The same tally shows who held the market up while it was away. Over those 6 weeks local institutions absorbed about RM1.59b of the foreign selling, or 57%, and retail investors about RM1.19b, or 43%. Last week, as foreign money returned, local institutions sold a net RM368m, which is to say foreign and retail buyers were largely taking stock off domestic funds.

**Where the gap can be**

There are only 4 ways a foreign investor can own a piece of Malaysia's export boom, and the flows show which ones have been chosen. The first is ringgit assets without company risk, meaning government bonds and deposits, which set a record in August and whose September figure arrives in early October. The second is locally listed shares, the one channel that has been net sold, visible every Monday in the weekly tally. The third is direct ownership of the plants and data centres doing the exporting, which surfaces in the balance of payments alongside third-quarter GDP in mid-November. The fourth is the shares of the foreign parent companies that own many of those plants, priced every night in New York; Intel, which runs large operations in Penang and Kedah, was among Monday's biggest gainers.

**The second order**

Of the roughly RM59.7b by which August exports grew on a year earlier, electrical and electronic products accounted for RM36.9b, or about 62%. My read is that a large part of that value is earned by plants whose owners are listed abroad, while the heavyweights named in Monday's session reports were 3 banks, a utility and a hospital group. The export boom strengthens the ringgit and Malaysia's credit standing, which is what a bond buyer is paid for, but it reaches listed earnings only indirectly, through bank lending, electricity demand and wages. If that holds, the foreign selling of local equities is less a vote against the data than a choice of instrument: foreigners have bought the story through the currency and the bond market and left the shares to local hands.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-22/chart-02-august-export-growth-by-product.png)

**What to do with this if you run a business here**

The same concentration test works on our own revenue. Take the last 12 months of invoiced revenue, tag each customer by whether its own business depends on electronics, semiconductors or data centres, and compute that group's share of this year's revenue growth, not of total revenue. If more than half of our growth traces back to that one chain, we are running the national export profile at company scale, and that is worth knowing before the next budget round rather than after it.

**What would change this view**

If foreign equity inflows continue for another 4 weeks and recover at least half of the RM2.78b, around RM1.4b, by the end of October while bond buying holds up, the instrument-choice reading is wrong and what we are watching is an equity re-rating that simply lagged the data. Renewed selling alongside a September bond outflow would point the other way, towards doubt about the story itself.

**What to watch**

- **Tue 22 Sep**: Iran's president at the UN General Assembly; any meeting with President Trump moves oil first.
- **Thu 24 Sep**: Trump–Xi summit in Washington, with the tariff truce and AI cooperation on the agenda.
- **Mon 28 Sep**: the next weekly foreign fund-flow tally, and the first test of whether last week's return repeats.
- **Early October**: Budget 2027 is tabled, and the central bank's September foreign bond-holdings data follows.
- **Mid-November**: third-quarter GDP and the balance of payments, where direct-investment flows show up.

Money tends to follow where returns are actually paid, which is not always where the headline is written. The question for our own plans is whether the growth we are counting on is growth we own or growth we only sit next to. Hold that thought until the flows print on 28 September.
