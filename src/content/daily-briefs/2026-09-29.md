*4-minute read*

**The Gist**

- Prices charged by Malaysian producers jumped 10.7% in August compared with a year earlier, up from 9.7% in July.
- Prices at the shops rose just 1.9% over the same year, so somebody along the way is soaking up the difference.

Follow a price rise from the ground to the shop shelf in August, and it gets smaller at every stop:

- Raw materials, like crude oil and gas straight out of the ground: up 24.8% on a year ago
- Half-made stuff that factories sell to other factories: up 9.6%
- Finished goods leaving the factory: up 3.1%
- What you actually paid at the shops: up 1.9%

The first three stops come from the Producer Price Index (PPI), which the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) released yesterday. Producer prices are what producers, like mines and factories, get paid when they sell what they make. The last stop is August's consumer price index, released on September 18, so that one isn't new. The wider economy looks decent (it grew 6.0% from April to June, and exports jumped 45.5% in August from a year earlier). But costs at the start of the supply chain are climbing much faster than anything you'd notice buying that glass of teh tarik.

![](/images/daily-briefs/2026-09-29/price-chain-aug-2026-2026-09-29.png)

**The top of the chain is where things got hot**

Mining producer prices rose 41.2% in August from a year ago, up from 30.5% in July. Crude petroleum extraction alone was up 49.8%. Further along, refined petroleum products like fuel rose 33.5% and electronics 12.4%.

Brent, the global oil price most headlines quote, settled at US$105.28 a barrel yesterday for November delivery, up 0.9%. That's background only, since the August numbers were counted well before it.

**The number that matters: 8.8**

Take producer inflation (10.7%), subtract consumer inflation (1.9%), and you get a gap of 8.8 percentage points. In July it was 7.9, so the gap got wider.

One honest caveat though: producer prices track what Malaysian producers get paid, including for goods sold abroad. While consumer prices track what households pay at the shops, where some prices such as fuel are set or subsidised. So the two measure different baskets.

That 8.8 points is sitting with someone

The numbers point to four places it could be:

- Businesses in the middle. Finished goods rose only 3.1% while raw materials rose 24.8%, so companies along the chain may be eating part of the rise in thinner margins.
- Foreign buyers. The biggest jumps are in mining and crude petroleum, and a lot of that is sold overseas.
- The government. Fuel is partly priced under the Automatic Pricing Mechanism (APM), the weekly pump-price formula, and partly subsidised, so some of that 33.5% may never reach the shop-price basket.
- You, later. Producer prices can take months to show up at the shops.

The data doesn't say which one is doing the heavy lifting. It could be more than one.

**Our read (this part is interpretation)**

If businesses in the middle are absorbing the cost, their margins are thinner than a 1.9% inflation figure suggests. If part of the gap is just a delay, some of it could still reach shop prices in coming months. That's a possibility, not a forecast.

Budget 2027 on October 9 touches two of those places. It sets fuel subsidy spending, and Economy Minister Akmal Nasrullah Mohd Nasir said yesterday that any move to a RM2,000 minimum wage rests with the Prime Minister when he tables it, with the impact on small businesses to be weighed. He didn't link that to producer prices, and neither do we.

**A quick check for your own business**

Line up your three biggest input costs against the closest DOSM category for August: refined petroleum up 33.5%, electronics up 12.4%, agriculture up 4.6%, manufacturing overall up 8.8%. Then see whether your supplier prices over the past year rose by more or less. A cost rising slower than its category may still have catching up to do.

**What would change this view**

A September consumer inflation reading well above 1.9% (due in October) would point to delay rather than absorption. A narrower gap in DOSM's September PPI would end the widening story.
