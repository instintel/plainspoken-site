*2-minute read*

On Tuesday, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) raised its growth forecast for Malaysia and four neighbours, Indonesia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand, to 4.4% this year as a group, from 4.1% in July. The IMF is the global body that lends to governments and forecasts every country's growth, meaning how much more an economy produces and earns than the year before.

![Photo of rows of computer servers in a data centre.](/images/daily-briefs/2026-10-14/photo-imf-growth-forecast-2026-10-14.jpg)

At home, last Friday's Budget 2027 papers forecast 4.8% to 5.3% growth for Malaysia in 2026, and August's exports, the goods Malaysia sells abroad, were 45.5% higher than a year earlier. Growth decides how much work and business goes around.

The IMF's reason is AI. It calls the tech boom the main thing holding growth up despite conflicts between countries, and expects several Southeast Asian economies to gain from building data centres, buildings packed with computers that run AI. It also names Malaysia, with South Korea, Thailand and Taiwan, as the four economies that sell the most AI computer hardware abroad, after taking away what they buy, for the size of their economies.

That same day, Singapore-based DayOne got another RM15 billion in bank loans to build more data centres in Johor.

Still, the IMF assumes oil stays expensive: about US$96 a barrel this year and about US$87 next year, with the Strait of Hormuz, the sea route out of the Gulf, only reopening from March 2027. So the weekly full price of petrol, and what the government pays to keep BUDI95 cheap, stay under pressure. And if AI spending slows, the IMF's main support for growth goes with it.

Friday, Oct 16 brings the first hard test, Malaysia's growth figure for July to September. Will it keep up?
